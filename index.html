Recent Works

  • The Real Effect of Investor Disagreement Job Market Paper Abstract
    This paper examines how investor disagreement affects the informational feedback from stock prices to corporate investment. We show that higher investor disagreement is associated with stronger investment-to-Q sensitivity. The effect is more pronounced when managers have greater demand for external information (i.e., for R\&D investment and among growth firms) and when investors’ information is more effectively incorporated into prices (i.e., under stronger investor price conditioning and higher analyst coverage), providing evidence consistent with managerial learning. Additional tests indicate that the results are not explained by market timing or valuation uncertainty. Disagreement constructed from analysts' forecast revisions around earnings announcements yields similar results and traces the information flow from the market back to the firm, and analyses exploiting Regulation Fair Disclosure as an exogenous shock provide causal evidence. Overall, the findings suggest that investor disagreement can reflect heterogeneous external information that prices aggregate and managers subsequently use in investment decisions.
    Presentations: BAFA Annual Conference (Scheduled Apr. 2026), Adam Smith Business School PhD Brownbag Seminar (Oct. 2025)
  • Investor Sentiment, Institutional Ownership, and Informational Efficiency of Prices Abstract
    We show that a stock's sensitivity to investor sentiment, measured by sentiment beta, significantly weakens the positive relation between institutional ownership and price efficiency. This impact of sentiment beta is more pronounced following optimistic periods, among overpriced stocks, and for active institutional ownership, confirming that sentiment beta acts as a binding arbitrage friction. We further show that this risk operates through two channels. First, only the sentiment-beta-orthogonal component of institutional ownership improves price efficiency, while the sentiment-beta-driven component does not. Second, institutions reduce both their holdings and trading in low-sentiment-beta stocks when sentiment exposure rises, precisely where their trading is most effective at reducing noise. Together, these channels create a self-reinforcing dynamic in which rising sentiment beta increases arbitrage risk, institutions disengage, and affected stocks are left with less informed participation and lower price efficiency. Our results are robust to alternative measures of efficiency, sentiment, and institutional activity, empirical specifications, and tests for reverse causality.
    Presentations: SFA Annual Meeting (Nov. 2025), FMA Europe Meeting (Jun. 2025), EFMA Annual Meeting (Jun. 2025)
  • ESG Investing and Revelatory Price Efficiency Abstract
    This study investigates the relation between ESG institutional ownership and revelatory price efficiency. Higher ESG institutional ownership is associated with greater sensitivity of corporate investment to Tobin's $Q$, consistent with stronger managerial learning from stock prices. The relation is particularly pronounced for R\&D investment, where learning is more needed. Higher ESG institutional ownership is associated with greater broad information acquisition and dissemination. This broader informational role is complemented by an ESG-specific component. The effect of ESG ownership is particularly evident when investors' ESG orientation concerns financially material sustainability issues and becomes more pronounced when climate-related opportunities are more salient. Overall, ESG institutional investors help incorporate information into stock prices that managers do not already possess and can use in investment decisions.
    Presentations: SFA Annual Meeting (Nov. 2025), Adam Smith Business School PhD Brownbag Seminar (Nov. 2024)
    Note: An earlier version of this paper was circulated under the title “ESG Investing and Stock Price Informativeness.”
